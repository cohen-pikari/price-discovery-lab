# Private Equity Valuation

## Why It's Hard to Price

Private equity stakes don't trade on a continuous market. There's no daily quote, no order book, no visible bid-ask spread. A fund typically holds a position for years between transactions, so between the entry price and an eventual sale or listing, the "value" of the stake exists only as an estimate — usually the fund manager's own mark, reported quarterly, based on judgment rather than observed trades.

That creates two structural problems. First, **valuations are infrequent and judgment-heavy** — a manager's quarterly mark can lag real economic conditions by months. Second, **there's an inherent conflict of interest**: the manager reporting the value is often the same party whose own performance fees depend on that value being favourable. Neither problem has a clean fix; the industry manages them through standardised guidelines (below) and external audit, not by eliminating the judgment involved.

## Market Context & Sizing

Precise, disaggregated figures for "private equity deal activity within Australian aviation" specifically aren't available through free public sources — that kind of granular breakdown typically sits behind paid industry data providers (Preqin, PitchBook). What is publicly documented, and sufficient for sizing the kind of transaction this sector sees, is the deal case used below: a single large, well-reported Australian aviation buyout, entry to exit.

Rather than estimate a market-wide "bubble" from incomplete public data, this investigation sizes one real, fully-documented transaction instead — which is actually the more honest approach when the aggregate figure isn't legitimately available.

## Standard Methodology

Private equity fair value is generally governed by the [IPEV (International Private Equity and Venture Capital) Valuation Guidelines](https://www.privateequityvaluation.com/Valuation-Guidelines), intended to represent current best practice for reporting private capital investments at fair value. The guidelines point toward a small number of standard approaches, typically used in combination and cross-checked against each other:

- **Market approach** — applying multiples (e.g. EV/EBITDA) from comparable listed companies or precedent private transactions to the company's own earnings.
- **Income approach** — a discounted cash flow (DCF) model, with a discount rate reflecting the business's risk and an illiquidity premium for the fact the stake can't be sold on demand.
- **Calibration to a recent transaction price** — when a company has recently raised capital or changed hands at a known price, that transaction price is used as the starting anchor, adjusted forward over time for changes in performance and market conditions since.

In practice, a manager will usually triangulate between these rather than relying on one method alone, and will disclose which approach(es) were used for a given valuation.

## Worked Example: Bain Capital's Acquisition of Virgin Australia (2020–2025)

This is a real, fully-documented Australian transaction, not a hypothetical.

**Entry (2020):** Virgin Australia entered voluntary administration in April 2020. Bain Capital's winning bid was reported, via administrator Deloitte's Report to Creditors, at a **$3.5 billion valuation**. The bid's structure: full repayment of roughly $450 million owed to staff, full repayment of $2.3 billion in secured debt, honouring travel credits, and existing shareholders (including Singapore Airlines, Etihad, and Virgin Group) receiving nothing. Unsecured creditors, owed around $2 billion, recovered only 9 to 13 cents on the dollar. ([Executive Traveller](https://executivetraveller.com/news/revealed-bain-s-3-5-billion-bid-for-virgin-australia); [Financier Worldwide](https://www.financierworldwide.com/bain-to-acquire-virgin-australia-but-creditors-try-to-block-deal))

This is a clean illustration of why a distressed-acquisition entry price is not simply "what the business is worth" — it's a function of the capital structure being restructured. Bain wasn't pricing the whole enterprise fresh; it was pricing what remained after secured creditors and staff were made whole and unsecured creditors and equity were wiped out.

**Value creation (2020–2025):** Over roughly five years as a private company under Bain's ownership, Virgin Australia was restructured and returned to profitability, setting up a public re-listing.

**Exit (2025):** Virgin Australia relisted on the ASX via IPO, raising approximately $685 million by selling 236.2 million shares at $2.90 each, valuing the airline at **$2.32 billion on a fully-diluted basis**. Bain's shareholding was reduced to 39.4% (retaining a majority private-equity-style position post-listing rather than fully exiting), with Qatar Airways holding 23%. ([Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/news/investing/virgin-australia-seeks-2-3-billion-valuation-at-ipo-reports/))

**What this shows about pricing mechanics:** the entry valuation ($3.5bn) and the exit valuation ($2.32bn) aren't directly comparable numbers — the 2020 figure describes a distressed restructuring (what the business was worth once its liabilities were cleaned up), while the 2025 figure is a fresh, market-tested price discovered through an actual IPO bookbuild, the closest thing to "real" price discovery an illiquid asset can get. The IPO is effectively the market finally pricing the asset directly, after five years of Bain's internal marks being the only available estimate.

## Limitations & Open Questions

- Bain's own internal marks for Virgin Australia between 2020 and 2025 aren't public — only the entry and exit prices are observable. Everything in between was private, judgment-based valuation, exactly the problem this investigation is about.
- The entry price reflects a distressed, post-restructuring capital structure, not a "normal" market transaction — it's a useful example of PE pricing mechanics, but not representative of valuing a healthy going concern.
- Without access to Preqin/PitchBook-style aggregated data, this investigation can document one real deal in depth but can't size the broader Australian aviation PE market with confidence. That's a genuine limitation of working from free public sources, not a gap to paper over.
